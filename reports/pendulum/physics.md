# Pendulum, sensors, and conditional reference

This is an exactly specified **discrete stochastic simulation** of a damped pendulum. It is not a hardware dataset or an exact solver for the continuous-time stochastic differential equation. Nondimensional time is measured in units of the pendulum's characteristic time. Each forecast step is 0.25 time units; by default it uses eight kick–drift substeps of duration 0.03125. For independent standard normals ξ and the known schedule value γ held constant over each macro interval, update

```text
ω ← ω + (−sin θ − γ ω) Δt + 0.36 sqrt(Δt) ξ
θ ← θ + ω Δt
```

Initial angles are uniform on [−π,π] and angular velocities independently follow N(0,1.2²). All schedule profiles, stop times and sensor coefficients are fixed in [the simulator](../../experiments/pendulum_system.py). Held-out smooth and abrupt test profiles are distinct from training and validation profiles but remain within the training damping range. Sensor maps A/B share the first three channels: cos θ, sin θ, and tanh(ω/2) in the full view; the third channel is sin(2θ) in the partial view. Five redundant nonlinear channels change their mixing coefficients. Thus testing a new sensor map tests nuisance-channel transfer **with calibrated anchors**, not invariance to an arbitrary new observation system.

At origin zero partial observations reveal angle but not velocity. Each independent reference/calibration branch independently resamples hidden velocity from its initial conditional law. Full-state branches fix both angle and velocity. Noise increments are always independent across continuations. This partial-observation rule would generally be invalid for later forecast origins; the new time-dependent conditional velocity law would have to be estimated or sampled independently.

For R independent reference replications of a bounded endpoint at a particular start, the sample-mean variance contributes s²/R in expectation. Subtract s²/R from each squared prediction error before averaging across starts; the result is unbiased for mean conditional-mean squared error, including when predictors share the same simulations for paired comparisons. Retain the signed estimate: taking its square root after clipping at zero gives a descriptive RMSE, not an unbiased quantity or a finite-sample confidence bound. For an exact alternative, average products of residuals over distinct continuation pairs; a test verifies equality. Precision and statistical guarantees depend on the independent-start count, not the number of overlapping endpoints.

**Numerical resolution.** A check on 1,024 starts, eight macro steps (2 time units), and the held-out switching law uses common Brownian increments across grids. Paired terminal observable RMS differences are 0.01289 (8 vs 16 substeps), 0.00643 (16 vs 32), and 0.01923 (8 vs 32). Noise-free endpoint errors against a separately integrated fourth-order Runge–Kutta reference with 128 substeps are 0.02577, 0.01283 and 0.00640 respectively. These values quantify discretization on one representative system; they are neither global error bounds nor a correction to a model comparison in the defined discrete benchmark. Numerical changes of this order can matter when interpreting small performance differences as continuous-time physical improvements. Reproduce with `python experiments/pendulum_system.py`; [fixed output](physics.json).
